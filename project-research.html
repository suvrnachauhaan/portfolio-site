Research First, Always

Led research that challenged the brief. Sometimes the best move is reframing the problem.

Overview

The brief said: "Redesign the dashboard." After two weeks of research, I came back with a different recommendation: don't redesign the dashboard — kill half of it. The data showed that 60% of the dashboard widgets were never interacted with. Users weren't asking for a prettier dashboard. They were asking for less.

This project is about what happens when you let research lead instead of assumptions.

Dashboard — Before & After Comparison
The Problem

Stakeholders were convinced the dashboard needed a visual refresh. But NPS comments told a different story: "too many widgets," "can't find what matters," "information overload." The dashboard had become a dumping ground for every metric anyone had ever requested.

The real problem wasn't aesthetics — it was information architecture and feature bloat.

User Interview — Affinity Map
Process

Quantitative: Pulled 90 days of analytics. Mapped click rates, hover time, and scroll depth for every dashboard widget. 12 out of 20 widgets had less than 5% interaction rate.

Qualitative: Conducted 15 user interviews across 3 user personas. Used card sorting to understand mental models. Users consistently grouped information differently than the dashboard presented it.

Reframing: Presented findings to stakeholders. Instead of "redesign the dashboard," the new brief became "build a focused command center." Reduced widgets from 20 to 8, reorganized by user task rather than data source.

Validation: A/B tested the new dashboard against the old. The new version had 40% less content but 3× more meaningful interactions.

Reflection

The hardest part of research isn't doing it — it's having the confidence to let the data change the plan. Stakeholders came in wanting a facelift. I gave them a strategy. That's what research-led design looks like: not just validating ideas, but sometimes invalidating them.
